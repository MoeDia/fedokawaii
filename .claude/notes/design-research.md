# Design research — the sources behind the design system

Date: 2026-11-01. Written for the owner's request, in his words: "do proper professional japanese
master style 100+ sources proper professional books and reputable world class designers for OS
and UX and UI for the alignment and measurements every piece of tui text bar size menu placement
text placement icon placement relative positioning relative human experience readability sizes
and contrast etc etc EVERY SINGLE THING NEEDS TO BE PERFECTLY SUPER ALIGNED".

**Purpose.** Ground the rules of `docs/design-system.md` (§2 Type, §3 Grid, §4 Parts, §5 States,
§7 Text screens, §11 Motion, §13 Icons) in real, reputable sources, and find where the system
falls short of them. 138 sources in eight groups (A 19, B 21, C 25, D 19, E 19, F 11, G 13,
H 11). The last section, "The rules for fedokawaii", turns them into 43 numbered rules and says
for each whether the design system already follows it, follows it only in part, contradicts it,
or does not say; it ends with the five places where the system falls shortest.

**Method.** Every title, author, link and ISBN below was checked on 2026-11-01 against a search
result or a page fetched that day (a book: the publisher's or a library's page, or the ISBN as
that page printed it). "What it says" is a paraphrase of what the page or its snippets stated,
never a quotation; where a page could not be read as text, or a figure was computed here (APCA
Lc, angular size, frame budget), the entry or the "Caveats" section at the end says so. The
desktop it is applied to: one 1920 × 1080 144 Hz monitor (24 inch class, EDID 53 × 30 cm, about 92 ppi) at scale 1,
an 8 × 20 px cell grid, Sumi UI (a 14 px pixel face) and Sumi Term (a 16 px coding face), pixel
icons at 12, 14, 16 and 28 px, two moods (dark and light).

**Entry format.** `- **Title** — author/organisation, year/edition — link — what it says that
matters — **rule:** the concrete rule we take.`

## A. Grids, proportion, alignment and typography (19)

- **Grid Systems in Graphic Design** — Josef Müller-Brockmann, Niggli, 1981 (reissued) — https://niggli.ch/en/grid-systems-in-graphic-design.html — ISBN 978-3-7212-0145-1. A page is divided into fields of one size with a fixed gap between them, and type size and leading are chosen so that every field holds a whole number of lines; the grid is the way text and picture lock to one rhythm. — **rule:** one module for the whole desktop (8 px column, 20 px row, gap 8); every width a whole number of columns and every height a whole number of rows; a deviation is written down as an exception (the dial).
- **The Elements of Typographic Style** — Robert Bringhurst, Hartley & Marks, 4th ed. 2012 (v4.0 2013) — https://search.worldcat.org/title/The-elements-of-typographic-style/oclc/967670384 — ISBN 978-0-88179-212-6. Typographic space is handled like musical rhythm and proportion; a single-column measure of about 45 to 75 characters is satisfactory (66 the ideal); marks that are optically off the line (hanging punctuation, round letters overshooting) are corrected by eye, not by number. — **rule:** a text panel's measure is 45 to 75 characters (ours: 47 cells; text-heavy screens cap at 75 columns); align by the ink, not by the box, wherever the ink differs (icons beside capitals, a dial's pictures).
- **The New Typography (Die neue Typographie)** — Jan Tschichold, trans. Ruari McLean, University of California Press, 1998 (2006 ed.) — https://www.ucpress.edu/books/the-new-typography/paper — ISBN 978-0-520-25012-3. Asymmetric composition, one sans-serif face and hierarchy made by contrast of size, weight and position rather than ornament. — **rule:** hierarchy by ink, place and rule weight only; no decoration that carries no meaning.
- **The Form of the Book** — Jan Tschichold, trans. Hajo Hadeler, ed. Robert Bringhurst, Hartley & Marks, 1991 (pbk 1997, ISBN 978-0-88179-116-7) — https://catalog.nypl.org/search/i9780881791167 — The text block's proportion and margins are derived from the page by a fixed construction (the margins grow from the inner edge to the bottom, which is the largest). — **rule:** pad is proportional and consistent per surface; the bottom margin of a panel is never smaller than the top (ours: 16 all round).
- **Detail in Typography** — Jost Hochuli, Hyphen Press, 2008 revised ed. — https://hyphenpress.co.uk/detail_in_typography_available_again/ — ISBN 978-0-907259-34-3. Letter-spacing and word-spacing are judged by the white between the shapes, so that a line reads as an even grey; a word space that is too wide breaks the line into holes. — **rule:** the word space is one glyph cell wide (7 px in Sumi UI, 8 in Sumi Term); every pair is judged by the white between letters (0 of the 8836 ASCII pairs may touch, §2), also in digits and symbols.
- **Practical Typography — Summary of key rules** — Matthew Butterick, online (practicaltypography.com) — https://practicaltypography.com/summary-of-key-rules.html — Body text 10–12 pt in print or 15–25 px on screen; line spacing 120–145 % of the point size; line length 45–90 characters including spaces; page margins control line length. — **rule:** line height 120–145 % of the size: 20 px on 14 px is 143 %, on 16 px is 125 %; both inside the range.
- **Thinking with Type** — Ellen Lupton, Princeton Architectural Press, 3rd ed. 2024 (ISBN 978-1-7972-2682-8; 2nd ed. 2010, ISBN 978-1-56898-969-3) — https://shop.posterhouse.org/thinking-with-type-3rd-edition.html — Type is set on a baseline grid with a small, deliberate scale of sizes; letters, text and grids are treated as one system. — **rule:** one baseline per row, one text size per face, display only at a whole multiple.
- **Graphic Design: The New Basics** — Ellen Lupton and Jennifer Cole Phillips, Princeton Architectural Press, 2nd ed. 2015 — https://search.icpl.org/Record/1409580 — ISBN 978-1-61689-332-3. Chapters on rhythm and balance, figure and ground, hierarchy, modularity, grid and time and motion present layout as a handful of reusable relationships. — **rule:** every surface is built from the same few parts (§4); figure and ground are one ink against one ground, never three.
- **Typographie: A Manual of Design** — Emil Ruder, Niggli, 1967 (new edition, English "Typography") — https://www.niggli.ch/en/produkt/typography/ — ISBN 978-3-7212-0043-0. The Basel teacher treats the white (counters, spacing, the page) as equal to the black and builds legibility from texture, weight and spacing. — **rule:** air is designed and measured as carefully as ink: every gap in a surface is a value in §3.
- **Making and Breaking the Grid** — Timothy Samara, Rockport, 2nd ed. 2017 — https://catalog.nypl.org/record=b22228409~S1 — ISBN 978-1-63159-284-3. Names the manuscript, column, modular and hierarchical grids and shows that a grid is broken on purpose, in one place, to give emphasis. — **rule:** a surface that leaves the 8 × 20 grid (the dial's 304 px square) says so in §3 and keeps every other edge on the grid.
- **Designing Programmes** — Karl Gerstner, Lars Müller Publishers, 1964 (facsimile 2019) — https://www.lars-mueller-publishers.com/designing-programmes — ISBN 978-3-03778-578-2. Design is a programme: fix the rules and the variables first, and each piece follows from them. — **rule:** `docs/design-system.md` is the programme; a surface is built from its numbers and tested against them (`tests/static/test_design_doc.sh`).
- **More Meaningful Typography** — Tim Brown, A List Apart no. 327, 2011 — https://www.alistapart.com/article/more-meaningful-typography — Sizes are taken from a modular scale (a ratio from music, geometry or nature) built on values that matter to the content. — **rule:** if a second text size is ever needed it is a ratio of the base (the display size is exactly 2 ×, never 1.5 or 1.75).
- **Compose to a Vertical Rhythm** — Richard Rutter, 24 ways, 12 December 2006 — https://24ways.org/2006/compose-to-a-vertical-rhythm/ — The line height is the unit of vertical space; paragraph margins, headings and borders are made multiples of it (his example: 12 px text, 1.5 line height, an 18 px unit), and a border is paid for out of its padding so the rhythm survives. — **rule:** a rule or border sits inside its row (our heading rule is the row's pixel row 10), never adds height; every vertical size is n × 20 px.
- **Geometry of Design: Studies in Proportion and Composition** — Kimberly Elam, Princeton Architectural Press, 2001 (rev. ed. 2011, ISBN 978-1-61689-036-0) — https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/mathematical-gazette/article/geometry-of-design-by-kimberly-elam-pp-107-soft-cover-1700-2001-isbn-1-56898-249-6-princeton-architectural-press-distributed-by-birkhauser-springerverlag/1C962F224036E9DF2F7C33B1A265FA17 — ISBN 978-1-56898-249-6 (1st ed.). Analyses well-known designs as constructions on a few ratios (root rectangles, the golden section). — **rule:** the pictures we draw (lock scenes, faces, crests) are composed on simple stated ratios (1:1, 2:3, 3:4, 16:9) and their centres fall on the grid's.
- **Stop Stealing Sheep & Find Out How Type Works** — Erik Spiekermann (first edition with E. M. Ginger), Adobe Press, 3rd ed. 2013 — https://www.peachpit.com/store/stop-stealing-sheep-find-out-how-type-works-third-edition-9780321934284 — ISBN 978-0-321-93428-4. Explains legibility through letter shape, counters and spacing, and warns against letter-spacing lowercase text or centring by habit. — **rule:** no letter-spacing is added to lower-case words; text is flush left, values flush right, nothing centred without a reason.
- **Designing Type** — Karen Cheng, Yale University Press, 2nd ed. 2020 (1st ed. 2006, ISBN 978-0-300-11150-7) — https://yalebooks.yale.edu/9780300249927/designing-type — ISBN 978-0-300-24992-7. Its closing chapters cover letter sidebearings, testing spacing, word space, numbers and punctuation, and kerning. — **rule:** spacing is tested on strings of real pairs, and digits and punctuation are tested as thoroughly as letters; tabular digits are one width.
- **Letters of Credit: A View of Type Design** — Walter Tracy, David R. Godine, 2004 pbk (first 1986) — https://typography.guru/directory/books/letters-of-credit-r26/ — ISBN 978-1-56792-240-0. A type designer's account of how letters are drawn and spaced for text sizes, and why the spacing of a face must be tuned by eye. — **rule:** a pixel face is corrected by eye at its real size on the real surface, not only by pair tables.
- **Verdana and Georgia — design intent** (message from Bill Hill, Microsoft Typography, to the W3C www-font list, 25 February 1998) — https://lists.w3.org/Archives/Public/www-font/1998JanMar/0047.html — Matthew Carter drew bitmaps at the main screen sizes first and then hinted outlines to reproduce those bitmaps; Verdana was given extra letter-spacing for screen readability. — **rule:** a screen face is drawn at its target pixel size first and spaced more openly than a print face (this is how Sumi UI was repaired).
- **On Slight Hinting, Proper Text Rendering, Stem Darkening and LCD Filters** — Nikolaus Waxweiler (corrections Graham Asher), FreeType documentation, 2015 — https://freetype.org/freetype2/docs/hinting/text-rendering-general.html — "Slight" hinting snaps vertically only and keeps the horizontal spacing; at small sizes grey coverage makes text look thin unless blending is gamma-correct, and stem darkening restores weight only when gamma correction is on. — **rule:** Sumi Term is rendered grey with light hinting (as §2 says) and its light-on-dark weight is checked on the dark mood at 16 px; Sumi UI is never smoothed.

## B. Japanese design masters, Japanese layout and Japanese aesthetics (21)

- **Designing Design (デザインのデザイン)** — Kenya Hara, Lars Müller Publishers, 2007 — https://www.artbook.com/9783037784501.html — ISBN 978-3-03778-450-1 (pbk). Essays and cases on emptiness as a designed quality, on making the everyday new again (the RE-DESIGN project) and on design for the senses; layout by Naoto Fukasawa Design. — **rule:** an empty area is a designed element, not a leftover: the size of every void in a panel is a stated multiple of the cell, and nothing is added to "fill" it.
- **White (白)** — Kenya Hara, Lars Müller Publishers, 2009/2010 — https://www.lars-mueller-publishers.com/white — ISBN 978-3-03778-183-8. Treats white as the origin of Japanese aesthetics, linked to paper and emptiness (the character for white is a part of the character for emptiness). — **rule:** the light mood's paper is one paper; the "white" of the page is the ground of everything and carries no second cream frame (§4 "never a second frame inside").
- **Kenya Hara on Muji: "not just about minimalism and simplicity"** — Dezeen interview, 13 December 2017 — https://www.dezeen.com/2017/12/13/kenya-hara-exclusive-interview-muji-us-expansion-brand-aesthetic/amp/ — Hara separates emptiness (a receptacle that the user fills with meaning) from minimalism (less for its own sake). — **rule:** at rest a surface is calm and nearly empty; it fills with meaning only when a state asks (a busy icon moves, a meter reddens, a notice appears).
- **Tanaka Ikko: Graphic Master** — Gian Carlo Calza with Ikko Tanaka, Phaidon, 1997 — https://search.worldcat.org/title/tanaka-ikko-graphic-master/oclc/38845470 — ISBN 978-0-7148-3716-1. Surveys Tanaka's work from the late 1950s: Japanese traditional motifs recast in modernist geometry, flat colour fields and strict grids; Tanaka also helped found Muji's concept and handed its art direction to Hara in 2001. — **rule:** tradition enters as geometry and flat ink (the seal as a clean disc, the kamon crests at 28 px), never as texture or gloss.
- **Yusaku Kamekura: His Works** — Yusaku Kamekura, Bijutsu Shuppan-sha, Tokyo, 1971 (foreword Herbert Bayer; essay Masaru Katsumie) — https://modernism101.com/?p=17191 and the AGI profile https://a-g-i.org/design/1964-tokyo-olympics — The 1964 Tokyo Olympics' design director, a follower of the International Typographic Style, blended Japanese aesthetics with geometric forms and strong photography; the Olympic posters won the Ministry of Education's Grand Prize. — **rule:** one strong mark, one red, geometric purity: the vermilion is a disc or a field with clean edges, used once per surface for the thing that matters.
- **The 1964 Tokyo Olympic pictograms** — Masaru Katsumie (art director) and Yoshiro Yamashita, with the Nippon Design Center — https://www.olympics.com/ioc/news/developing-a-new-global-visual-communication-language and https://ndc.co.jp/en/projects/olympic-games-tokyo-1964-graphic — The first Games-wide pictogram set (20 sport and 39 facility symbols) was made so visitors with no Japanese could read it: one construction, one weight, one logic for every sign. — **rule:** an icon set shares one grid, one stroke weight and one construction (1 px strokes on the em, §13); an icon is learned once and understood without words.
- **Requirements for Japanese Text Layout (JLREQ)** — W3C Internationalization Working Group, Working Group Note, 11 August 2020 — https://www.w3.org/TR/jlreq/ — Kanji and kana each sit in a square character frame and are set solid by default (§2.1.2–2.1.3); a quarter em of space sits between Western letters or digits and Japanese characters (§3.2.6); the Western word space is a third of an em in principle (§3.2.2); a line's length is a whole multiple of the character size (§3.1.12). — **rule:** Japanese text sits on its square frame (14 px in Sumi UI, two 8 px cells in Sumi Term); a Latin word next to kana or kanji gets about a quarter em of air; a line of Japanese is a whole number of characters wide.
- **JIS X 4051:2004 (日本語文書の組版方法) — as explained by JAGAT** — Japan Association of Graphic arts Technology — https://www.jagat.or.jp/past_archives/content/view/3509.html — The Japanese industrial standard that JLREQ is built on (established 1993, revised 1995 and 2004): how characters sit in lines, lines on a page and headings, figures, tables, running heads and folios are handled. — **rule:** where the Learn pieces set Japanese, the house rules are those of JIS X 4051 (line-head and line-end prohibitions, squeezed brackets), not Latin habits.
- **text-spacing-trim, text-autospace (CSS Text Level 4)** — MDN Web Docs on the W3C CSS Text Module Level 4 — https://developer.mozilla.org/en-US/docs/Web/CSS/text-spacing-trim — Adjacent CJK punctuation is trimmed to half-width gaps (text-spacing-trim, which needs the font's halt or chws features), and its companion text-autospace puts the space between ideographs and Latin letters or digits. — **rule:** in our surfaces (no CSS) the same effect is done by hand: adjacent Japanese brackets and stops are drawn half-width, and a Latin or digit run beside kana gets its quarter-em air.
- **UD書体 (Universal Design fonts)** — Morisawa — https://www.morisawa.co.jp/fonts/udfont/ — Morisawa's UD faces aim at being easy to recognise, easy to read and hard to misread, with medium-sized counters and a check that every character stays distinguishable at small sizes. — **rule:** at 14 px every kanji used in the UI is checked for being told apart from its neighbours (open counters, no filled-in strokes) before it is drawn into Sumi UI.
- **J Type 101** — Koyubi Studio, April 2025, 44 pp. — https://www.slanted.de/?p=806462 — A designer's English primer on Japanese typography as a system: horizontal and vertical setting, line breaks, spacing and the relation of Japanese and Latin type, in ten practical rules. — **rule:** the Latin–Japanese relation (baseline, optical size, spacing) is a property of the pair of faces, so it is tested in every real surface where they meet (the lock quote, the bar's day seal, the launcher).
- **CJKV Information Processing** — Ken Lunde, O'Reilly, 2nd ed. 2008/2009 — https://www.oreilly.com/library/view/cjkv-information-processing/9780596156114/ — ISBN 978-0-596-51447-1. Reference for how Japanese is encoded and displayed, including full-width and half-width forms and character classes. — **rule:** a Japanese character is two cells wide in every terminal screen and column counts use display width, never string length.
- **A Dictionary of Color Combinations** — Sanzo Wada, Seigensha Art Publishing, 2011 (from the 1930s six-volume original), vol. 1 — https://cca.qc.ca/en/search/details/library/publication/763320580 — ISBN 978-4-86152-247-5. 348 colour combinations drawn from Wada's 1930s work. — **rule:** colours are Wada's or a stated mix of two (§1); a combination is chosen from the book's own pairings before a new one is invented.
- **Wabi-Sabi: for Artists, Designers, Poets & Philosophers** — Leonard Koren, Stone Bridge Press, 1994 (ISBN 1-880656-12-4); Imperfect Publishing, 2008 (ISBN 978-0-9814846-0-0) — https://katalog.uth.edu.pl/cgi-bin/koha/opac-ISBDdetail.pl?bib=89022 — Wabi-sabi is a modest, unpretentious beauty of the imperfect, impermanent and incomplete. — **rule:** warmth and age in the palette (the dark mood's umber, the light mood's kinari), no fake gloss: no gradient, bevel or glow; but alignment is never the place for imperfection.
- **In Praise of Shadows (陰翳礼讃)** — Jun'ichirō Tanizaki, trans. Thomas J. Harper and Edward G. Seidensticker, Leete's Island Books, 1977 — https://www.cca.qc.ca/en/search/details/library/publication/46333462 — ISBN 978-0-918172-02-0. Beauty comes from half-light and shadow, from dim gold and lacquer that needs darkness; harsh, even light flattens it. — **rule:** the dark mood is dim, not black-and-white: gold is a dim metal, never bright; the brightest ink in the dark mood is `strong`, never #ffffff.
- **The Book of Tea** — Kakuzō Okakura, G. P. Putnam's Sons, 1906 — https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/769 — The tea-room is an "abode of vacancy" and of the unsymmetrical; beauty is left incomplete so that the viewer completes it. — **rule:** asymmetry and incompleteness are allowed where they are deliberate (a heading's rule that stops at the content edge, an unlit meter segment, a ghost word in a search line).
- **MA: Espace-temps du Japon** — exhibition by Arata Isozaki with Tōru Takemitsu, Musée des Arts Décoratifs, Paris, 11 October – 11 December 1978, programme of the Festival d'Automne — https://www.festival-automne.com/storage/medias/Publish__evenement__448__FAP_1978_AP_01_JP_PRGS.pdf — Introduced ma, the interval that joins time and space, to Western audiences. — **rule:** the interval between two things is designed first: gaps are 8 px (one cell) and 20 + 8 px under a heading, repeated everywhere and never "whatever is left".
- **Zen and the Fine Arts** — Shin'ichi Hisamatsu, trans. Gishin Tokiwa, Kodansha International, 1971 — https://terebess.hu/zen/mesterek/Zen-and-the-fine-arts.pdf (a scan of the book) and the review in The Eastern Buddhist https://otani.repo.nii.ac.jp/record/9321/files/EB5-1-12.pdf — Derives seven characteristics of Zen art from Zen itself, among them asymmetry, simplicity (kanso), austere sublimity, naturalness, subtle profundity, freedom from attachment and tranquility. — **rule:** kanso: remove until nothing can be removed without loss; every extra line, label or colour on a surface has to defend itself.
- **Japanese Aesthetics** — Graham Parkes and Adam Loughnane, Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy (rev. 2018, 2023) — https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/japanese-aesthetics/ — Surveys mono no aware, wabi, sabi, yūgen, iki and kire (the "cut"), with the tea aesthetics of Rikyū and the films of Ozu. — **rule:** kire: separations are clean cuts (a 1 px hairline, a gap), not gradients or blurs; no soft shadows beyond the stated two-row shadow.
- **The Beauty of Everyday Things** — Sōetsu Yanagi, Penguin Classics (Penguin Modern Classics in the UK), 2019 — https://penguinrandomhouse.com/books/608234/the-beauty-of-everyday-things-by-soetsu-yanagi — ISBN 978-0-241-36635-6. The mingei argument: beauty belongs to the useful, repeated, unsigned object made without ornament for ornament's sake. — **rule:** every drawn thing is also a working thing: art in a surface is a state or a place, never filler.
- **Registered features: palt (Proportional Alternate Widths)** — OpenType specification 1.8.x, Microsoft Learn — https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/typography/opentype/otspec182/features_pt — `palt` gives proportional advances to kana and Latin in Japanese fonts through GPOS adjustments, normally off by default. — **rule:** the fixed grid (full-width kana, 7 px letters) is our choice; any proportional Japanese setting is a deliberate exception per surface and must keep its columns on the cell grid.

## C. Interface masters, human-factors research and laws (25)

- **Ten principles of good design ("Good design")** — Dieter Rams, as published by Vitsœ, undated page (© 2026) — https://www.vitsoe.com/gb/about/good-design — Innovative, useful, aesthetic, understandable, unobtrusive, honest, long-lasting, thorough down to the last detail, environmentally friendly, as little design as possible ("less, but better"); nothing may be arbitrary or left to chance. — **rule:** no number on any surface is arbitrary: if a size, gap or colour is not in `docs/design-system.md`, it is a defect, not a choice.
- **Dieter Rams: As Little Design as Possible** — Sophie Lovell, foreword Jonathan Ive, Phaidon, 2011 (ISBN 978-0-7148-4918-8; rev. 2024, ISBN 978-1-83866-909-6) — https://www.phaidon.com/products/dieter-rams-as-little-design-as-possible — The monograph of Rams' Braun and Vitsœ work, where order and restraint make objects calm and long-lived. — **rule:** before adding a row, label or colour to a surface, remove one; the quietest version that still works is the design.
- **The Design of Everyday Things, revised and expanded** — Don Norman, Basic Books 2013 (ISBN 978-0-465-05065-9); MIT Press pbk 2014 (ISBN 978-0-262-52567-1) — https://jnd.org/books/the-design-of-everyday-things-revised-and-expanded-edition/ — Good design makes the possible actions discoverable (signifiers), gives feedback and keeps mappings natural. — **rule:** every state is visible where the thing is (on/off in the ink and the icon, not in a sentence); a control looks like what it does.
- **The Visual Display of Quantitative Information** — Edward Tufte, Graphics Press, 2nd ed. 2001 (ISBN 978-1-930824-13-3) — https://www.edwardtufte.com/book/the-visual-display-of-quantitative-information/ — Maximise the share of ink that carries data (data-ink ratio), erase non-data ink and chartjunk, use small multiples. — **rule:** a meter's unlit segments are the only "empty" ink; frames, rules and decoration are cut to the one hairline each part needs.
- **Designing Interfaces, 3rd ed.** — Jenifer Tidwell, Charles Brewer and Aynne Valencia, O'Reilly, 2020 — https://www.oreilly.com/library/view/designing-interfaces-3rd/9781492051954 — ISBN 978-1-4920-5196-1. A pattern catalogue (titled sections, visual framework, escape hatch, progress indicators, responsive disclosure). — **rule:** each part of a panel has one name (heading, row, key, meter, tab) and every surface uses that part the same way (§4).
- **Refactoring UI** — Adam Wathan and Steve Schoger, 2018 — https://www.refactoringui.com/ — Hierarchy comes from size, weight and colour used sparingly ("de-emphasise to emphasise"); define a spacing system up front; use fewer borders (separate with space or contrast instead); never rely on colour alone. — **rule:** one spacing scale (§3) fixed before drawing; inside a panel only headings carry a rule; every state also has a shape or a word, not colour alone.
- **10 Usability Heuristics for User Interface Design** — Jakob Nielsen (1994), updated by Kate Moran and Feifei Liu, Nielsen Norman Group, rev. 2024 — https://www.nngroup.com/articles/ten-usability-heuristics/ — Visibility of system status, consistency and standards, recognition rather than recall and aesthetic and minimalist design (leave out what is irrelevant or rare; extra content competes with what matters). — **rule:** every state change shows within 100 ms; one word means one thing everywhere; rarely needed information is behind one key, not on screen.
- **Response Times: The 3 Important Limits** — Jakob Nielsen, 1 January 1993 (from Usability Engineering) — https://www.nngroup.com/articles/response-times-3-important-limits/ — 0.1 s feels instantaneous (the result is the feedback), 1 s keeps the flow of thought, 10 s is the limit of attention; animations should run on a real-time clock, not processor speed. — **rule:** key or click to the first changed pixel within 100 ms (hover highlight within a frame, 7 ms at 144 Hz); anything over 1 s shows a busy icon; motion is driven by the presentation clock (§11 already).
- **Laws of UX** — Jon Yablonski, lawsofux.com (book: O'Reilly) — https://lawsofux.com/ — Collects Fitts's, Hick's, Miller's, Jakob's, proximity, common region, Prägnanz, the Doherty threshold (productivity rises when both sides answer within about 400 ms) and the aesthetic-usability effect. — **rule:** group by proximity and a common region (a panel), never by two cues at once; no animation may make the user wait near the 400 ms Doherty line (§6: "nothing waits for an animation").
- **The information capacity of the human motor system in controlling the amplitude of movement** — Paul M. Fitts, Journal of Experimental Psychology 47(6):381–391, 1954 — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/13174710 — Movement time depends on the distance to a target and its size (Fitts's law, later written T = a + b·log2(2D/W)). — **rule:** frequently used targets are large and near where the pointer rests; a hit area is never smaller than what is drawn; where a target is under 24 px in one direction (our 20 px rows and keys) it is as wide as the whole panel and the exception is written down (see WCAG 2.5.8 below).
- **On the rate of gain of information** — W. E. Hick, Quarterly Journal of Experimental Psychology 4(1):11–26, 1952, doi 10.1080/17470215208416600 (cited at https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hick%27s_law) — Choice reaction time grows with the logarithm of the number of equally likely alternatives. — **rule:** a menu shows few choices at once (Super+U tabs of about five); a long list gets a search line (§7), not scrolling.
- **The magical number seven, plus or minus two** — George A. Miller, Psychological Review 63(2):81–97, 1956, doi 10.1037/h0043158 — https://www.mpi.nl/publications/item2364276/magical-number-seven-plus-or-minus-two-some-limits-our-capacity-processing — Immediate memory is limited in chunks, not bits; recoding into chunks extends it. — **rule:** group long lists into named sections of a few rows, and name each group.
- **The magical number 4 in short-term memory** — Nelson Cowan, Behavioral and Brain Sciences 24(1):87–114, 2001 — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11515286/ — The working-memory limit is about four chunks, not seven. — **rule:** a state the eye must compare (the dial's lit hours, a row of tabs) stays within about four to five distinct things or is grouped.
- **Laws of Organization in Perceptual Forms** — Max Wertheimer, 1923 (English trans. in W. D. Ellis, A Source Book of Gestalt Psychology, 1938), Classics in the History of Psychology — https://psychclassics.yorku.ca/Wertheimer/Forms/forms — Dots are seen as groups by proximity and similarity. — **rule:** the gap inside a group is smaller than the gap between groups by at least 2 : 1 (our 8 px between keys against 20 + 8 px between sections).
- **The Psychology of Human-Computer Interaction** — Stuart K. Card, Thomas P. Moran and Allen Newell, 1983 (pbk ISBN 0-89859-859-1) — https://www.computerhistory.org/collections/catalog/102786305 — Introduces GOMS and the keystroke-level model (a keystroke about 0.2 s, a mental preparation step about 1.35 s). — **rule:** keyboard-first: every action is reachable in at most two keys, because each extra mental step costs more than the keys.
- **A Quiz Designed to Give You Fitts** — Bruce Tognazzini, AskTog, 1999 — https://asktog.com/columns/022DesignedToGiveFitts.html — The pointer stops at the screen edge, so a target on the edge is effectively infinitely deep; the first row of pixels must be clickable with no buffer zone. — **rule:** the bar's tiles take clicks all the way to the screen's top edge and corner (the 3 px of air above the hairline must not be dead).
- **Beyond Fitts' law: models for trajectory-based HCI tasks** — Johnny Accot and Shumin Zhai, CHI '97 (pp. 295–302) — https://www.chi1997.chistatic.hosting.acm.org/proceedings/paper/ja.html — The steering law: the time to move through a tunnel grows with its length over its width (T = a + b·A/W). — **rule:** a pointer path into a menu or dropdown is a wide tunnel: a popup opens directly under its tile with no gap (§6: top hairline on y 33) and overlapping its tile's width.
- **The Eight Golden Rules of Interface Design** — Ben Shneiderman, University of Maryland (from Designing the User Interface, 6th ed.) — https://www.cs.umd.edu/~ben/goldenrules.html — Strive for consistency, informative feedback, dialogs that yield closure, simple error handling, easy reversal, internal locus of control, reduce short-term memory load. — **rule:** every action is reversible or confirmed (power, screen off); the user is always the one who acts (§6 "nothing ever waits").
- **Designing with the Mind in Mind, 3rd ed.** — Jeff Johnson, Morgan Kaufmann/Elsevier, 2020 — https://shop.elsevier.com/books/designing-with-the-mind-in-mind/johnson/9780128182024 — ISBN 978-0-12-818202-4. Builds UI guidelines on perception and cognition (our perception is biased by experience, context and goals). — **rule:** every surface is judged in its real context (on its real ground, beside its neighbours, in both moods) before it is called aligned.
- **Magic Ink: Information Software and the Graphical Interface** — Bret Victor, 2006 — https://worrydream.com/MagicInk/MagicInk.pdf — Most information software should be context-sensitive information graphics; interaction is often a crutch for bad information design. — **rule:** show the answer without a click (the bar tile's state, the dial's middle names the item under the cursor); a control exists only where a choice does.
- **Designing Visual Interfaces: Communication Oriented Techniques** — Kevin Mullet and Darrell Sano, SunSoft Press/Prentice Hall, 1995 — https://www.nngroup.com/articles/foreword-visual-design/ (foreword by Jakob Nielsen) — ISBN 0-13-303389-9. Applies print visual-communication principles (economy, unity, simplicity) to GUIs. — **rule:** unity and economy as tests in the grill list: same frame, same grid, same ink roles; the fewest elements that say it.
- **The Humane Interface** — Jef Raskin, Addison-Wesley, 2000 — https://www.oreilly.com/library/view/humane-interface-the/0201379376/ — ISBN 978-0-201-37937-2. Quantifies interface efficiency with GOMS, Fitts and Hick, and argues against hidden modes. — **rule:** no hidden modes: a panel opens clean (no remembered cursor), and what a key does depends on what is shown.
- **Information Visualization: Perception for Design, 4th ed.** — Colin Ware, Morgan Kaufmann/Elsevier, 2019 — https://www.elsevier.com/books/catalog/isbn/9780128128756 — ISBN 978-0-12-812875-6. Perception-based design: what is seen pre-attentively (colour, motion, orientation) and how contrast and luminance carry shape. — **rule:** one pre-attentive channel per meaning: vermilion marks the thing that matters and nothing else; motion is reserved for busy states.
- **About Face: The Essentials of Interaction Design, 4th ed.** — Alan Cooper, Robert Reimann, David Cronin and Christopher Noessel, Wiley, 2014 — https://investors.wiley.com/news/news-details/2014/About-Face-The-Essentials-of-Interaction-Design-4th-Edition-2014-9-16/default.aspx — ISBN 978-1-118-76657-6. Goal-directed design: software should be polite, anticipate needs and never make the user feel stupid. — **rule:** smart, not chatty: do the obvious right thing (Steam's icon shows Steam, Super+/ knows the app), say nothing about it.
- **Don't Make Me Think, Revisited** — Steve Krug, New Riders, 3rd ed. 2014 — https://archive.org/details/dontmakemethinkr0000krug — ISBN 978-0-321-96551-6. Make every page self-evident; get rid of half the words, then half of what is left. — **rule:** no text that explains what the design makes obvious; labels are one or two words, lower case.

## D. Platform and design-system guidelines (19)

- **Human Interface Guidelines — Layout** — Apple, current — https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/layout — A consistent layout that adapts across display sizes helps people understand an app; Apple's design sessions add that hierarchy should come from layout and grouping rather than decoration, and that views are centred mathematically where that works and offset optically where it does not. — **rule:** hierarchy by position and grouping; centre by the ink (optical centre) wherever the shape is not a plain rectangle.
- **Human Interface Guidelines — Typography** — Apple, current — https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/typography — Text styles are fixed combinations of weight, size and leading, used consistently; Apple's WWDC 2019 session on text scaling gives 13 pt as macOS's default size (17 pt on iOS). — **rule:** a small set of named text styles; ours is one (Sumi UI 14 on 20, Sumi Term 16 on 20), and nothing ad hoc.
- **UI Design Dos and Don'ts** — Apple Developer, https://developer.apple.com/design/tips/ — Recommends at least 11 pt text, aligning text, images and buttons to show how information is related, and putting controls close to the content they change. — **rule:** controls sit next to what they change (a meter's value on its row, a key under its list); a row's value is right-aligned on the same edge down the panel.
- **Material Design 3 — Typography** — Google, current — https://m3.material.io/styles/typography/applying-type — Five roles (display, headline, title, body, label) in three sizes each, 15 styles; line height about 1.2 × the size for display to title and about 1.5 × for body and label. — **rule:** line height 1.2–1.5 × size: ours 1.43 (14 on 20) and 1.25 (16 on 20), inside the range.
- **GNOME Human Interface Guidelines** — GNOME Project, current — https://developer.gnome.org/hig/ — Design principles, guidelines (icons, input, typography, navigation, scaling, accessibility) and patterns in four groups (containers, navigation, controls, feedback) for GTK 4 and libadwaita. — **rule:** the same four families describe our parts (container = panel, navigation = tabs, control = key/meter/dial, feedback = notice/OSD); keyboard navigation and scaling are part of the design, not an afterthought.
- **KDE Human Interface Guidelines** — KDE Community, current — https://develop.kde.org/hig/ — "Simple by default, powerful when needed", layout and navigation, text and labels, icons, accessibility; guidance, not an ironclad law code, "under-promise and over-deliver". — **rule:** the default screen shows the minimum; the rest is one key away (Super+/, Tab).
- **elementary OS Human Interface Guidelines** — elementary, Inc., current — https://docs.elementary.io/hig/ — A living set of guidelines, not a rule book, covering the interface elements and how to combine them so an app belongs on the desktop. — **rule:** an app that is not ours (GTK, Qt, FireAlpaca) is fitted to the desktop by theme, font and spacing, not left to look foreign.
- **Fluent 2 — Layout** — Microsoft, current — https://fluent2.microsoft.design/layout — Base unit 4 px with a ramp of 0, 2, 4, 6, 8, 10, 12, 16, 20, 24, 28, 32, 36, 40, 48, 52, 56; gutters are multiples of the base unit; more space around an element makes it look more important; space groups related content, so dividers are often unnecessary. — **rule:** every gap is one of a fixed list (ours: 0, 1, 8, 16, 20, 28 px); group by space and drop the divider wherever space alone says it.
- **Typography in Windows** — Microsoft Learn, current — https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/windows/apps/design/signature-experiences/typography — Windows 11's type ramp sets body text at 14/20 effective pixels (a 14 px size on a 20 px line), body large at 18/24. — **rule:** our 14 on 20 is the same ratio as Windows 11's body text, so it needs no apology for density; the 1.43 line height is the proven choice.
- **Spacing (Carbon Design System)** — IBM, current — https://carbondesignsystem.com/elements/spacing/overview/ — Tokens at 2, 4, 8, 12, 16, 24, 32, 40, 48, 64, 80, 96, 160 px (multiples of 2, 4 and 8) that complement the 2x grid; avoid deviating from the scale; use the grid for horizontal spacing. — **rule:** a published spacing scale and a statement that deviations are exceptions; ours is §3 and must say so.
- **Spacing (Atlassian Design System)** — Atlassian, current — https://atlassian.design/foundations/spacing — 8 px base; tokens from 2 px to 80 px; small (0–8) for compact UI, medium (12–24) for larger components, large (32–80) for layout; related elements closer together than unrelated ones. — **rule:** three size classes of space (inside a part ≤ 8, around a part 8–20, between parts ≥ 20) written down with the 2 : 1 proximity ratio.
- **Spacing (GOV.UK Design System)** — Government Digital Service, current — https://design-system.service.gov.uk/styles/spacing/ — A scale in steps of 5 px (5, 10, 15, 20, 25, 30, 40, 50, 60) with responsive and static variants and only occasional overrides. — **rule:** spacing is chosen from named steps, never typed as a one-off number in a file.
- **Spacing settings and baseline-grid utilities (Vanilla framework)** — Canonical, v4.x — https://vanillaframework.io/docs/settings/spacing-settings and https://vanillaframework.io/docs/utilities/baseline-grid — Everything adheres to a 0.5 rem (8 px) baseline grid; because CSS cannot read font metrics, text is nudged onto the grid by measured per-size offsets. — **rule:** the nudge of text onto the baseline is measured per face and written down (ours: capitals on the row's rows 5–14 for Sumi UI, 4–15 for Sumi Term), then tested by a pixel probe, not by eye.
- **Spacing units (U.S. Web Design System)** — GSA/TTS, current — https://designsystem.digital.gov/design-tokens/spacing-units/ — Spacing tokens are multiples of 8 px with extra small tokens (4 px) and named large ones; any spacing in utilities or component CSS uses the tokens. — **rule:** same as Carbon: one token list, and "round up to the next token" when a legacy value falls between two.
- **Size primitives (Primer)** — GitHub, current — https://primer.style/product/primitives/size/ — Base sizes in px (2 up to 128, with 4 and 8 the common steps) and semantic gaps built on them (a condensed stack gap is 8 px). — **rule:** semantic names over raw numbers (gap, pad, indent) so a panel is described by roles.
- **Macintosh Human Interface Guidelines** — Apple Computer, Addison-Wesley, 1992 — ISBN 0-201-62216-5 — https://guidebookgallery.org/books/applehumaninterfaceguidelines — The classic statement of the Macintosh look and feel: consistency, direct manipulation, feedback, forgiveness, aesthetic integrity. — **rule:** aesthetic integrity: the design should look like it belongs to the job (a retro-Japanese desktop for a retro-Japanese owner), and consistency is paid for everywhere.
- **The Windows Interface Guidelines for Software Design** — Microsoft Press, 1995 — ISBN 1-55615-679-0 — https://guidebookgallery.org/books/thewindowsinterfaceguidelinesforsoftwaredesign — Rules and many worked examples for laying out dialogs, wizards, toolbars and context menus. — **rule:** an unambiguous dialog layout (labels aligned, buttons in a fixed place, default button marked) repeated in every panel.
- **Common User Access: Basic Interface Design Guide (SC26-4583-0)** — IBM, 1989 (see also https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IBM_Common_User_Access) — https://gavinhoward.com/uploads/CUA_Basic_Design.pdf — IBM's rules for panels, selection and keyboard interaction that defined many of the conventions desktops still follow. — **rule:** keys for the same job are the same in every screen (Esc closes, Enter acts, Tab walks) and need no footer (§7).
- **Icon Theme Specification** — freedesktop.org, current — https://specifications.freedesktop.org/icon-theme/latest/ — Icon directories are Fixed, Scalable (MinSize/MaxSize) or Threshold; a requested size picks the closest directory. — **rule:** ship each icon at its exact native sizes (12, 14, 16 and 28 px) and never let a toolkit scale one; an app icon is requested at its native 16 px (§3).

## E. Accessibility, legibility and colour (19)

- **Web Content Accessibility Guidelines (WCAG) 2.2** — W3C Recommendation, 12 December 2024 edition — https://www.w3.org/TR/WCAG22/ — 1.4.3: 4.5:1 for text (3:1 large); 1.4.6 (AAA): 7:1 (4.5:1 large); 1.4.11: 3:1 for the parts of controls, states and graphics needed to identify them; 1.4.12: content must survive line height 1.5, paragraph spacing 2, letter spacing 0.12 and word spacing 0.16 em; 2.4.7 focus visible; 2.4.11 focus not obscured; 2.4.13 (AAA) focus indicator of 2 px perimeter and 3:1. — **rule:** words ≥ 4.5:1 on every ground they sit on and the selected row's words ≥ 7:1 (as §1); icons and a meter's level ≥ 3:1; the keyboard cursor is always visible and is the strongest mark on screen.
- **Understanding SC 2.5.8: Target Size (Minimum)** — W3C WAI, WCAG 2.2 — https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/target-size-minimum.html — A pointer target is at least 24 × 24 CSS px unless it has enough clearance (a 24 px circle on it overlaps no other target), is inline, is duplicated by another control, or is essential. — **rule:** pointer targets are 24 px in both directions or their clearance exception is written down: our 20 px rows and keys are not, so they need that written exception (full panel width, keyboard-first) or 24 px.
- **APCA in a Nutshell** — Andrew Somers, Myndex Research — https://git.apcacontrast.com/documentation/APCA_in_a_Nutshell.html — The Accessible Perceptual Contrast Algorithm: Lc 90 preferred for fluent body text, Lc 75 minimum for body columns (16 px at weight 500 or 14 px at 700), Lc 60 for other content text, Lc 45 for large or heavy text, Lc 30 for placeholder and disabled text, Lc 15 for non-text; it says WCAG 2.x overstates contrast for dark colours. — **rule:** measure both WCAG ratio and APCA Lc; body words ≥ Lc 75 in both moods, `muted` ≥ Lc 60, `faint` ≥ Lc 30; the dark mood is judged by Lc first because the WCAG ratio flatters it.
- **Contrast and Color Accessibility** — WebAIM, last updated 9 January 2021 — https://webaim.org/articles/contrast/ — Ratios run 1:1–21:1, are not rounded (4.47:1 fails), large text is 18 pt (24 px) or 14 pt bold (18.7 px); for gradients, transparency and images test the lowest-contrast area; hover and focus states are tested on their own. — **rule:** a panel at kawa-glass's 0.92 is tested against the worst wallpaper pixel behind it, not against its flat ground; every hover, cursor and busy state is measured separately.
- **ISO 9241-303:2011 — Requirements for electronic visual displays** — ISO/TC 159/SC 4, 2nd ed., reviewed 2022 — https://www.iso.org/standard/57992.html — Image-quality requirements and recommendations for displays, independent of technology, task and environment, with the eyesight of older people in view. — **rule:** the display is part of the design: the monitor is set (brightness, gamma, white point over DDC/CI) so that the palette's measured contrasts hold on the real glass.
- **ISO 9241-125:2017 — Guidance on visual presentation of information** — ISO, edition 1, reviewed 2022 — https://www.iso.org/standard/64839.html — Guidance on how software presents information, dependent on the visual design approach, task, user, environment and technology; replaces ISO 9241-12:1998. — **rule:** a design decision is justified by the task and the environment it serves (a monitor at arm's length, long sessions, two moods), and then tested there.
- **Legibility of Print** — Miles A. Tinker, Iowa State University Press, 1963 (LCCN 63-16674; ISBN 0-8138-0990-8) — https://matdisc.unitech.ac.pg/Record/8137 (scan: https://www.gwern.net/doc/design/typography/1963-tinker-legibilityofprint.pdf) — Gathers Tinker's experiments (1927–1959) on type size, line width, leading, contrast and typeface and what each does to reading speed. — **rule:** change one variable at a time and measure reading speed; no size, spacing or weight change is accepted by eye alone.
- **Does print size matter for reading? A review of findings from vision science and typography** — Gordon E. Legge and Charles A. Bigelow, Journal of Vision 11(5):8, 2011, doi 10.1167/11.5.8 — https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3428264 — Fluent reading spans x-heights from about 0.2° to 2°; the critical print size for normal readers is about 0.2° x-height; angular size = 57.3 × size ÷ distance; x-height, not body size, is the better measure. — **rule:** keep every text's x-height ≥ 0.2° at the real viewing distance. Computed here for the 24 inch class 1080p monitor (EDID 53 × 30 cm, pitch about 0.275 mm): Sumi UI's 7-row x-height is 1.92 mm, i.e. 0.22° at 50 cm and 0.18° at 60 cm; Sumi Term's 8.7 px (x-height 1115 of 2048 units in the installed UDEV Gothic NF file) is 0.27° at 50 cm and 0.23° at 60 cm. Sumi UI is at the floor and must not shrink further.
- **Psychophysics of Reading in Normal and Low Vision** — Gordon E. Legge, Lawrence Erlbaum Associates, 2007 — https://eld.bl.uk/catalog/018865804 — ISBN 978-0-8058-4328-6. The book summarising Legge's programme on how contrast, size, spacing and font affect reading speed in normal and low vision. — **rule:** low-contrast, small text is the first thing to fail for a low-vision reader: the owner's `faint` ink is for what is not there yet, never for what must be read.
- **Reading Letters: Designing for Legibility** — Sofie Beier, BIS Publishers, 2012 — https://typography.guru/directory/books/reading-letters-r204/ — ISBN 978-90-6369-271-1. Reviews research on how letterforms are recognised, to help type designers draw legible faces and designers pick one; it does not prescribe sizes or leading. — **rule:** legibility is tested on the confusable letters (0 O, 1 l I, rn m, cl d) of each face at its real size on the real ground, not decreed.
- **The influence of reading speed and line length on the effectiveness of reading from screen** — Mary C. Dyson and Mark Haselgrove, International Journal of Human-Computer Studies 54(4):585–612, 2001 (summarised at https://humanfactors.com/newsletters/optimal_line_length.html) — Moderate lines of about 55 characters supported effective reading speed and comprehension on screen. — **rule:** running text in a panel is about 50–60 characters per line (ours: 47 cells for Sumi Term) and never more than 75.
- **Positive display polarity is advantageous for both younger and older adults** — Cosima Piepenbrock, Susanne Mayr, Ina Mund and Axel Buchner, Ergonomics 56(7), 2013 — https://documentacion.fundacionmapfre.org/documentacion/bib/143702.do — Dark text on a light ground gave better visual acuity and proofreading for both age groups. — **rule:** the light mood is the one for reading and long text; the dark mood must pay back its disadvantage with more contrast on small text.
- **Smaller pupil size and better proofreading performance with positive than with negative polarity displays** — Cosima Piepenbrock, Susanne Mayr and Axel Buchner, Ergonomics 57(11), 2014 — https://documentacion.fundacionmapfre.org/documentacion/publico/en/bib/150136.do — Pupils were smaller and proofreading better with positive polarity, consistent with a luminance explanation; a companion paper from the same group (Human Factors 56(5):942–951, 2014, tested at 8, 10, 12 and 14 pt) found the advantage grew as characters shrank. — **rule:** in the dark mood small text (14 px) gets the strongest inks and no thin `faint` words; check Sumi Term for gamma-correct blending or stem darkening so light-on-dark strokes do not look thin.
- **Dark Mode vs. Light Mode: Which Is Better?** — Raluca Budiu, Nielsen Norman Group, 2 February 2020 — https://www.nngroup.com/articles/dark-mode/ — Light mode (positive polarity) gave better performance, more so as text got smaller; dark mode may help some low-vision users; recommends offering dark mode as an option, especially for long reading, rather than as the sole default. — **rule:** both moods stay first-class (the owner's choice) and the switch is one key; the dark mood's small text is its hardest test.
- **Legibility, Readability, and Comprehension: Making Users Read Your Words** — Jakob Nielsen, Nielsen Norman Group, 15 November 2015 — https://www.nngroup.com/articles/legibility-readability-comprehension/ — Legibility is mainly typography (size, contrast, a plain background, a clean face); a design that is read about 20 % slower than a reference is probably poor. — **rule:** compare reading speed on a fixed text on the old and the new face or size; more than 10 % slower is rejected.
- **The effects of visual crowding, text size, and positional uncertainty on text legibility at a glance** — Dobres, Wolfe, Chahine and Reimer, Applied Ergonomics 70:240–246, 2018 (MIT AgeLab) — https://agelab.mit.edu/transportation-and-livable-communities/projects/glance-based-legibility — Studies text read in a glance, where crowding, size and uncertainty about position decide legibility. — **rule:** the bar and the level display are read at a glance: their spacing is looser than a paragraph's and their positions never move.
- **A perceptual color space for image processing (Oklab)** — Björn Ottosson, 23 December 2020 — https://bottosson.github.io/posts/oklab/ — A simple space in which lightness, chroma and hue can be changed independently; blends pass through believable intermediates and blue does not drift to purple as in CIELAB or HSV. — **rule:** colours are mixed in Oklab only (as §1 does); never lighten or mix in HSL or sRGB.
- **The science of color & design (HCT)** — Material Design 3, Google — https://m3.material.io/blog/science-of-color-design — In HCT a tone difference of 40 guarantees a WCAG ratio of at least 3:1 and a difference of 50 at least 4.5:1 (as the glossary puts it), so contrast is picked by tone. — **rule:** build the ink ladder on tone steps (words ≥ 50 tone apart from their ground, icons and meters ≥ 40) so both moods keep contrast when the palette changes.
- **Interaction of Color, 50th anniversary edition** — Josef Albers, Yale University Press, 2013 (foreword Nicholas Fox Weber; ISBN 978-0-300-17935-4) — https://www.davidzwirner.com/collect/josef-albers-interaction-of-color-book — A colour is never seen alone: its look depends on its neighbours and ground (simultaneous contrast). — **rule:** judge every ink on every ground it will be drawn on (as the contrast table does), on the real screen, in both moods.

## F. Terminal and TUI craft (11)

- **Bubble Tea** — Charm, github.com/charmbracelet/bubbletea (MIT) — https://github.com/charmbracelet/bubbletea — A Go TUI framework on the Elm architecture (a model, `Init`, `Update`, `View`) with a cell-based renderer; the whole screen is described by `View` and the framework redraws. — **rule:** a screen is a pure function of its state; only what changed is drawn again (§11), and state never lives in the drawing.
- **Lip Gloss** — Charm, github.com/charmbracelet/lipgloss (MIT) — https://github.com/charmbracelet/lipgloss — Layout vocabulary for terminals: padding and margin per side, borders (normal, rounded, thick), alignment, fixed width and height, join horizontal/vertical, place a block in a larger space, and colours chosen for light or dark backgrounds. — **rule:** describe every text screen in the same few verbs (pad, border, align, join, place) and give each a number in cells (margin 1 cell, pad 2, gap 1).
- **Textual — Layout guide** — Textualize, textual.textualize.io — https://textual.textualize.io/guide/layout/ — Layouts are vertical, horizontal, grid, dock and layers; sizes are integers in cells, `fr` shares or percentages; a grid's gutter of "1 2" looks even because a terminal cell is about twice as tall as wide. — **rule:** spacing in a terminal is optical: one blank line is about two columns of air, so a vertical gap of n lines pairs with a horizontal gap of 2n columns (our 8 × 20 cell makes that ratio 2.5).
- **Ratatui — Layout concepts** — ratatui.rs — https://ratatui.rs/concepts/layout/ — Areas are split by constraints (Length, Min, Max, Percentage, Ratio, Fill) solved with Cassowary; the solution is approximate when constraints conflict, and leftover space goes to the last area unless a `Min(0)` takes it; `spacing(n)` inserts gaps. — **rule:** fixed parts are fixed in cells, one part is allowed to flex (the list), and leftover space is assigned deliberately, never by default.
- **Unicode Standard Annex #11: East Asian Width** — Ken Lunde (editor), Unicode 18.0.0, revision 46, 2026-07-31 — https://www.unicode.org/reports/tr11/ — Defines F, H, W, Na, A, N; Wide and Fullwidth are two cells, Ambiguous resolves by context (narrow when unsure) and the annex says terminals need to be tailored case by case. — **rule:** a terminal column is counted by display width with Ambiguous = 1 cell; Sumi Term's icons are made two cells wide on purpose (Sumi Icons Term), and no pixel icon relies on an ambiguous-width codepoint.
- **UTF-8 and Unicode FAQ for Unix/Linux** — Markus Kuhn, University of Cambridge — https://www.cl.cam.ac.uk/~mgk25/unicode.html — Programs must tell byte count, character count and display width apart: CJK ideographs take two columns, combining marks none, measured with `wcwidth()`/`wcswidth()`. — **rule:** every TUI that aligns columns measures with wcswidth (kawa's width tables); a string's length is never a column count.
- **ECMA-48: Control Functions for Coded Character Sets, 5th ed.** — Ecma International, June 1991 (ISO/IEC 6429) — https://ecma-international.org/wp-content/uploads/ECMA-48_5th_edition_june_1991.pdf — The standard behind ANSI escape sequences: cursor movement, erasing, graphic renditions. — **rule:** a screen is drawn with standard sequences only; no behaviour that works in foot but breaks in the console or a multiplexer.
- **Command Line Interface Guidelines** — Aanand Prasad, Ben Firshman, Carl Tashian and Eva Parish — https://clig.dev/ — Human-first design; say just enough; respond within about 100 ms; use colour sparingly and disable it when not a TTY or when NO_COLOR is set; skip animations when output is not a TTY; messages to stderr. — **rule:** every TUI answers within 100 ms (a spinner or a state before any wait), never animates into a pipe, and keeps colour to meaning.
- **NO_COLOR** — informal standard, 2017 — https://no-color.org/ — When `NO_COLOR` is present and non-empty, software must not add ANSI colour; user config and flags may still override per program. — **rule:** TUIs honour NO_COLOR with a readable monochrome (state still shown by shape: `●`, `○`, bold, hollow meter segments).
- **Terminal colours are tricky** — Julia Evans, 1 October 2024 — https://jvns.ca/blog/2024/10/01/terminal-colours/ — The 16 ANSI colours have no standard values and defaults give low contrast (blue on black, yellow on white); programs that hard-code 256-colour values or backgrounds clash with the user's palette; a terminal "minimum contrast" feature rescues bad pairs. — **rule:** our 16 colours are ours (§1) and each is checked on `ground` and on `key`; TUIs use the 16 slots, not hard-coded 256-colour numbers.
- **Notcurses** — Nick Black, github.com/dankamongmen/notcurses (Apache 2.0) — https://github.com/dankamongmen/notcurses — Draws graphics from Unicode blitters (quadrants, sextants, octants, Braille) and, when the terminal reports support, real bitmaps through Sixel or the Kitty protocol; planes can move, hide and be transparent; glyph widths must match `wcswidth()`. — **rule:** pictures inside a TUI are made from half-blocks or bitmaps at an exact whole-cell size, and are tested for width against wcswidth.

## G. Pixel art craft and bitmap fonts (13)

- **Pixelblog (64 tutorials)** — Raymond Schlitter (SLYNYRD) — https://www.slynyrd.com/pixelblog-catalogue — Free tutorials from colour palettes built in HSB (no. 1) and light and shadow (no. 6) to UX/UI basics (no. 26: health bars and gauges), animation and tiles. — **rule:** pixel art is drawn from one small fixed palette with hue-shifted ramps (shadow toward cool or warm, never just darker); our art uses the Wada tokens only.
- **Pixel Art Tutorials** — Pedro Medeiros (Saint11), 512 × 512 px sheets — https://saint11.art/blog/pixel-art-tutorials/ — A free index of sheets including Outlines, Shading, Subpixel, Easings, Silhouette and UI 9-slice. — **rule:** every icon reads as a silhouette at 12 px before any detail is added; panels are 9-slices that grow in whole cells; movement is eased in whole pixels (§11).
- **Pixel Art Tutorial: Basics** — Derek Yu, derekyu.com (updated 28 January 2020) — https://www.derekyu.com/makegames/pixelart.html — Outlines are one pixel thick; jaggies (stray pixels that break a line's consistency) are minimised; anti-aliasing only where the background is known (never on a sprite's outer edge); dithering sparingly; "if you squint, a few large clusters of light and dark should still emerge". — **rule:** one run length per stair, no stray pixels, no anti-aliasing on outer edges, dither only on large flat areas; squint-test every picture.
- **Pixel Logic: A Guide to Pixel Art** — Michael Azzi (Michafrar), about 240 pp., revised 2022 — https://michafrar.gumroad.com/l/pixel-logic — An image-led guide from line art to fully animated sprites, built on analysed game examples. — **rule:** line first, then fill, then light, then animation: no step is skipped and each is reviewed at 1× and at 3×.
- **Pixel Art for Game Developers** — Daniel Silber, CRC Press/Routledge, 2015 — https://www.routledge.com/Pixel-Art-for-Game-Developers/Silber/p/book/9781482252309 — ISBN 978-1-4822-5230-9. A practical manual in more than fifty short chapters on drawing pixel art for games. — **rule:** draw at the final pixel size; nothing is ever drawn large and scaled down, or small and scaled up.
- **Susan Kare's Macintosh icons and fonts** — Smithsonian Magazine, "How Susan Kare Designed User-Friendly Icons for the First Macintosh" — https://www.smithsonianmag.com/innovation/how-susan-kare-designed-user-friendly-icons-for-first-macintosh-180973286/ and the Stanford "Interview with Susan Kare" https://web.stanford.edu/dept/SUL/sites/mac/primary/interviews/kare/trans.html — Kare drew the Mac's icons on 16 × 16 and 32 × 32 grids by hand, in black and white without anti-aliasing, and made Chicago from horizontals, verticals and 45-degree angles to tame the jagged look of bitmap text. — **rule:** glyphs and icons are built from horizontals, verticals and clean 45° stairs only; an icon is a familiar object, not an abstraction.
- **Glyph Bitmap Distribution Format (BDF) Specification, version 2.2** — Adobe Systems, 22 March 1993 — https://adobe-type-tools.github.io/font-tech-notes/pdfs/5005.BDF_Spec.pdf — A plain-text bitmap font format with integer metrics (FONT_ASCENT, FONT_DESCENT, a bounding box and device width per glyph). — **rule:** a bitmap face is defined by integers: Sumi UI is 14 rows (ascent 12, descent 2 with the baseline under row 11), 7 px a letter, 14 a kanji; any layout number derived from it is an integer too.
- **Misaki font (美咲フォント)** — Num Kadoma, littlelimit.net; Debian package fonts-misaki — https://packages-pkgmirror-csail.debian.org/sid/fonts-misaki — An 8 × 8 Japanese Gothic and Mincho bitmap face originally for the PC-E500 pocket computer and later converted to TrueType. — **rule:** Japanese at very small sizes needs a face drawn natively on its pixel grid; scaled outlines turn kanji to mush.
- **Terminus Font** — Dimitar Zhekov, v4.49.1 — https://terminus-font.sourceforge.net/ — A clean fixed-width bitmap font made for people who spend eight or more hours a day at a computer; 1,356 characters over about 120 language sets. — **rule:** a terminal face is chosen for an eight-hour day: the look-alikes (0 O, 1 l I |) are checked in every face we ship.
- **Unscii** — Viznut; unscii-8 (8 × 8) and unscii-16 (8 × 16) as described at https://pkgsrc.se/fonts/unscii — Bitmapped Unicode fonts for character-cell art and terminals, based on the C64, Amiga, Amstrad CPC and IBM PC ROM fonts and Atari arcade fonts, with block and PETSCII graphics at their Unicode positions. — **rule:** block and box characters used as art come from one source so seams line up; in foot they are drawn by foot itself, so pictures keep to whole cells.
- **Fusion Pixel Font** — TakWolf, github.com/TakWolf/fusion-pixel-font (font OFL-1.1, tools MIT) — https://github.com/TakWolf/fusion-pixel-font/ — Pan-CJK pixel font in 8, 10 and 12 px assembled from Ark Pixel (10, 12), Misaki (8 px Japanese kanji) and others, shipped as OTF, TTF, BDF and PCF. — **rule:** each size has its own drawing; a face is used only at the sizes it was drawn for (a 12 px face is not run at 14).
- **DotGothic16** — Fontworks Inc., 2020, on Google Fonts (SIL OFL 1.1) — https://en.fontworks.co.jp/news/2021/01/15/9531/ — A Japanese pixel Gothic modelled on classic 16 × 16 bitmaps, released to Google Fonts in January 2021. — **rule:** at 16 px it is on its native grid; our 14 px Sumi UI is on Shinonome 14's native grid; neither is ever scaled.
- **Shinonome fonts (東雲フォント)** — 12-, 14- and 16-dot Japanese bitmap fonts, public domain (plan9port README) — https://pkgsrc.se/fonts/ja-shinonome and https://plan9port.googlesource.com/plan9/+/refs/heads/master/font/shinonome/README — The Japanese bitmap font family that JF Dot Shinonome Gothic 14, Sumi UI's base, takes its name from. — **rule:** the family is public domain, so every repair of its derivative that we make is allowed and is documented (the JIS-sign fixes, the column of air, the Latin pairs).

## H. Motion and timing (11)

- **Easing and duration (tokens)** — Material Design 3, Google — https://m3.material.io/styles/motion/easing-and-duration/tokens-specs — Duration bands: short 50–200 ms, medium 250–400, long 450–600, extra-long 700–1000; emphasized decelerate cubic-bezier(0.05, 0.7, 0.1, 1.0), emphasized accelerate (0.3, 0, 0.8, 0.15), standard decelerate (0, 0, 0, 1), standard accelerate (0.3, 0, 1, 1). Google now calls these legacy in favour of springs. — **rule:** things that enter decelerate and things that leave accelerate; durations come from a short ladder (ours: 60, 120, 160, 200, 240, 300, 420, 1200 ms).
- **Motion** — Apple Human Interface Guidelines — https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/motion — Fluid motion conveys status, gives feedback and instruction, and enriches the experience; Apple's accessibility criteria say decorative motion may stop when the user prefers reduced motion but meaningful motion (a status change) must not vanish. — **rule:** a "reduce motion" switch removes the dissolves, scrolls and washes but keeps status changes (a cut, a colour, a frame of the icon).
- **Designing Fluid Interfaces (WWDC18, session 803)** — Apple — https://developer.apple.com/videos/play/wwdc2018/803/ — Start with no overshoot; bounce only when it is purposeful, for example when a gesture carries momentum. — **rule:** no overshoot anywhere (§11 eases out only): the stamp's 1 px swell is a press, not a bounce.
- **Executing UX Animations: Duration and Motion Characteristics** — Page Laubheimer, Nielsen Norman Group, 9 February 2020 — https://www.nngroup.com/articles/animation-duration/ — Simple feedback about 100 ms, substantial changes 200–300 ms, general range 100–400 ms (400 only for big movements on large screens); entering slightly longer than leaving (300 in, 200–250 out); ease-out for entering, ease-in or ease-in-out for leaving; avoid linear motion. — **rule:** out is shorter than in (ours: popups 200/160, notices 240/200, windows 420/400); no linear motion; a 420 ms window or workspace move is at the top of the research range and is justified only by its size.
- **The Illusion of Life: Disney Animation** — Frank Thomas and Ollie Johnston, Abbeville Press, 1981 (ISBN 0-89659-233-2); Disney/Hyperion, rev. 1995 (ISBN 0-7868-6070-7) — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Illusion_of_Life — The twelve principles (squash and stretch, anticipation, staging, straight-ahead and pose-to-pose, follow-through and overlapping action, slow in and slow out, arcs, secondary action, timing, exaggeration, solid drawing, appeal) from Disney's animators. — **rule:** a stamp has its anticipation (1 px larger) and its settle; staging means one action at a time on screen; overlapping the leaving and arriving windows (out 0–280, in 140–420) is follow-through.
- **The Animator's Survival Kit, expanded edition** — Richard Williams, Faber & Faber, 2009 — https://www.faber.co.uk/product/9780571238347-the-animators-survival-kit/ — ISBN 978-0-571-23834-7. A working manual of timing and spacing: how the distance between successive drawings shapes weight, ease and impact. — **rule:** each animation is specified as a spacing chart in frames at 144 Hz (frame numbers and positions), not only as milliseconds; 420 ms is 60.5 frames, so say 60 or 61.
- **Designing Interface Animation: Meaningful Motion for User Experience** — Val Head, Rosenfeld Media, 2016 — https://www.rosenfeldmedia.com/books/designing-interface-animation/ — ISBN 978-1-933820-32-3. Motion should orient, give feedback, show status or add personality, each with a stated purpose. — **rule:** every motion has one stated purpose (orient, confirm, show status, or delight) in §11, and delight is the only one that may be removed.
- **Motion, Tweening, and Easing (chapter 7 of Programming Macromedia Flash MX)** — Robert Penner (the easing equations are MIT/BSD licensed) — https://fox-gieg.com/patches/flash/advice/penner_chapter7_tweening.pdf — The source of the standard quad, cubic, quart and other ease-in, ease-out and ease-in-out functions. — **rule:** one named family for every ease (cubic-out for movement, smoothstep for a dissolve's threshold), applied to whole-pixel positions.
- **Animation: From Cartoons to the User Interface** — Bay-Wei Chang and David Ungar, UIST '93, pp. 45–55 — https://courses.ischool.berkeley.edu/i247/s08/readings/Chang_AnimationInUI_UIST93.pdf — Applies cartoon animation principles to the user interface so that the user can follow what changes; received the UIST Lasting Impact Award in 2004. — **rule:** objects stay traceable through a change: a window that rises 9 px while dissolving is the same object; nothing appears or vanishes without a path.
- **Principles of Traditional Animation Applied to 3D Computer Animation** — John Lasseter, Computer Graphics 21(4):35–44 (SIGGRAPH '87), doi 10.1145/37401.37407 — https://www.evl.uic.edu/aej/527/papers/lasseter.pdf — Carries squash and stretch, staging, timing and secondary action from hand-drawn animation to computer animation, with personality as the goal. — **rule:** staging: the eye is led to one moving thing at a time; secondary motion (the stamp, the beads) never competes with the primary (the window).
- **Measure performance with the RAIL model** — Google, web.dev — https://web.dev/articles/rail — Finish a response within 100 ms (handle input within 50 ms); at 60 Hz a frame is 16 ms but only about 10 ms is your own budget; do idle work in slices of 50 ms or less. — **rule:** at 144 Hz a frame is 6.94 ms; by the same 60 % proportion our own work per frame is about 4 ms (computed here); input is handled within 50 ms; the dwl frame log checks both.

## The rules for fedokawaii

Forty-three rules for a 1920 × 1080, 144 Hz, scale-1 desktop (24 inch class panel, pixel pitch about
0.275 mm; an 8 × 20 px cell; Sumi UI 14 px, Sumi Term 16 px; icons 12/14/16/28 px; two moods).
Each rule names its sources by title (section A–H above) and ends with one line on
`docs/design-system.md`: **follows**, **partly**, **contradicts** or **does not say**.

### Grid, spacing and alignment

1. **One module for everything: 8 × 20 px.** Every width is a multiple of 8 and every height n × 20 plus multiples of 8; each exception is listed with its reason. Sources: *Grid Systems in Graphic Design*; *Designing Programmes*; *Making and Breaking the Grid*; *Spacing (Carbon Design System)*; *Spacing (Atlassian Design System)*; *Spacing settings and baseline-grid utilities (Vanilla framework)*. Design system: **follows** (§3 cell and its stated exceptions: dial, stamp, meter, icons); no formula yet for a panel's total height (rows × 20 + k × 8).
2. **Spacing comes from a closed, named list; a number outside it is a defect.** Sources: *Fluent 2 — Layout*; *Spacing (Carbon Design System)*; *Spacing (GOV.UK Design System)*; *Spacing units (U.S. Web Design System)*; *Size primitives (Primer)*; *Ten principles of good design*. Design system: **partly** (§3 names gap 8, pad 16, indent 8, heading 20 + 8, but other values live elsewhere: 3 px of air, 4/12 px between tiles, 14 px in the clock, 2/4 px dial insets; the list is not declared closed).
3. **Proximity: the space inside a group is at most half the space between groups** (8 px inside, 28 px between). Sources: *Laws of Organization in Perceptual Forms*; *Laws of UX*; *Spacing (Atlassian Design System)*; *Fluent 2 — Layout*. Design system: **does not say** (the numbers already satisfy it: keys 8 apart, sections 20 + 8 apart).
4. **Vertical rhythm: every row 20 px; rules and borders sit inside a row and add no height.** Sources: *Compose to a Vertical Rhythm*; *Refactoring UI*; *Spacing settings and baseline-grid utilities (Vanilla framework)*. Design system: **follows** (§3 row 20; the heading rule on the row's pixel row 10; §4 no inner rule).
5. **Align by ink, not by box** (icons on the capitals' axis, pictures by their ink, caps centred in the row, five rows of paper above and below). Sources: *The Elements of Typographic Style*; *Human Interface Guidelines — Layout*; *Stop Stealing Sheep & Find Out How Type Works*; *Spacing settings and baseline-grid utilities (Vanilla framework)*. Design system: **follows** (§3 words in a row, §4 key and dial, §8 bar rows).
6. **A popup centred on the screen sits at the optical centre (a little above the geometric one); a panel's bottom pad is never smaller than its top.** Sources: *The Form of the Book*; *Human Interface Guidelines — Layout*. Design system: **does not say** (§6 "centred on the screen"; pad is 16 all round, which is fine).
7. **A text measure of 45–75 characters, about 55 best; text-heavy screens cap their width.** Sources: *The Elements of Typographic Style*; *Practical Typography — Summary of key rules*; *The influence of reading speed and line length on the effectiveness of reading from screen*. Design system: **partly** (§3 content is 47 cells; nothing caps notes, Learn pieces or torrents in a wide window).
8. **Line height 120–145 % of the size.** Sources: *Practical Typography — Summary of key rules*; *Material Design 3 — Typography*; *Typography in Windows*. Design system: **follows** (§2: 20 on 14 is 143 %, 20 on 16 is 125 %; Windows 11's own body text is 14 on 20), the reason is not written down.
9. **Hierarchy by ink, place and rule only; one size per face; any display size an exact multiple.** Sources: *The New Typography*; *Thinking with Type*; *More Meaningful Typography*; *Refactoring UI*. Design system: **follows** (§2 "One size for words", display ×2 only).
10. **Pointer targets are 24 × 24 px or a written exception; edge targets reach the screen edge.** Sources: *Understanding SC 2.5.8: Target Size (Minimum)*; *A Quiz Designed to Give You Fitts*; *The information capacity of the human motor system in controlling the amplitude of movement*; *Beyond Fitts' law: models for trajectory-based HCI tasks*. Design system: **contradicts** (§3 rows and keys are 20 px high, no exception written; §8's 3 rows of air above the bar's hairline are not said to take clicks).

### Type, legibility and colour

11. **Letter-fit is judged by the white between shapes; no pair touches; digits, symbols and punctuation are tested like letters; numbers are tabular.** Sources: *Detail in Typography*; *Designing Type*; *Letters of Credit: A View of Type Design*; *Typographie: A Manual of Design*; *Verdana and Georgia — design intent*. Design system: **follows** (§2: 0 of 8836 ASCII pairs touch, signs drawn, tabular numbers); evenness of the white (rhythm) is not measured, only touching.
12. **Pixel faces are used only at the size they were drawn for: no scaling, no hinting, no synthetic bold; the coding face grey with light hinting.** Sources: *Glyph Bitmap Distribution Format (BDF) Specification, version 2.2*; *Misaki font*; *Fusion Pixel Font*; *DotGothic16*; *Shinonome fonts*; *On Slight Hinting, Proper Text Rendering, Stem Darkening and LCD Filters*. Design system: **follows** (§2).
13. **Legibility floor: every text's x-height is at least 0.2° at the real viewing distance** (angular size = 57.3 × size ÷ distance). Computed here: Sumi UI's 7-row x-height (1.92 mm) is 0.22° at 50 cm and 0.18° at 60 cm, Sumi Term's 8.7 px is 0.27° and 0.23°; so 14 px is the floor and words never go smaller. Sources: *Does print size matter for reading? A review of findings from vision science and typography*; *Psychophysics of Reading in Normal and Low Vision*; *Legibility of Print*; *Legibility, Readability, and Comprehension*. Design system: **does not say** (no viewing distance, no angular size, no minimum x-height).
14. **Small light-on-dark text gets more contrast than the ratio asks: body words at least APCA Lc 75, secondary words Lc 60, faint Lc 30.** Computed here with the APCA-W3 constants: dark `text` Lc 68, dark `muted` Lc 38, dark `accent_text` Lc 38 (all below those floors although their WCAG ratios are 10.0, 5.1 and 4.9:1); light mood `text` Lc 86, `muted` Lc 64, `accent_text` Lc 62. Sources: *APCA in a Nutshell*; *Positive display polarity is advantageous for both younger and older adults*; *Smaller pupil size and better proofreading performance with positive than with negative polarity displays*; *Dark Mode vs. Light Mode: Which Is Better?*; *Contrast and Color Accessibility*. Design system: **contradicts** by APCA (§1 judges the dark mood by the WCAG 2 ratio alone, which APCA says overstates dark colours); it follows WCAG.
15. **WCAG contrast: words 4.5:1, selected rows 7:1, non-text 3:1, no rounding up; hover, cursor and busy states measured on their own.** Sources: *Web Content Accessibility Guidelines (WCAG) 2.2*; *Contrast and Color Accessibility*. Design system: **follows** (§1 table; `tools/contrast.py` measures every selected state).
16. **A translucent ground is tested against the worst pixel behind it.** Sources: *Contrast and Color Accessibility*; *Legibility, Readability, and Comprehension*. Design system: **does not say** (§1 measures flat tokens although panels are kawa-glass at 0.92).
17. **Colours are mixed in Oklab, the ink ladder is built on tone steps (words at least 50 tone from their ground, icons 40), every ink is checked on every ground.** Sources: *A perceptual color space for image processing (Oklab)*; *The science of color & design (HCT)*; *Interaction of Color*. Design system: **partly** (§1 states Oklab mixes and a per-ground table; no tone-step rule).
18. **Any change of face, size or weight is accepted only if reading speed on a fixed text stays within 10 % of the old.** Sources: *Legibility of Print*; *Legibility, Readability, and Comprehension*; *The influence of reading speed and line length on the effectiveness of reading from screen*. Design system: **does not say**.
19. **Japanese beside Latin: about a quarter em of air between Latin letters or digits and kana or kanji, a square character frame, half-width squeeze for adjacent brackets and stops, lines of Japanese a whole number of characters.** Sources: *Requirements for Japanese Text Layout (JLREQ)*; *text-spacing-trim, text-autospace (CSS Text Level 4)*; *JIS X 4051:2004*; *J Type 101*; *CJKV Information Processing*; *Registered features: palt*. Design system: **does not say** (§2 only gives kana and kanji a column of air at their left).

### Icons, art and the Japanese principles

20. **One construction for every icon (one grid, one 1 px stroke, one ink per state) at exact native sizes 12, 14, 16 and 28, never scaled.** Sources: *The 1964 Tokyo Olympic pictograms*; *Susan Kare's Macintosh icons and fonts*; *Icon Theme Specification*; *Pixel Art for Game Developers*. Design system: **follows** (§3, §13, §2 "no scaling anywhere").
21. **Strokes are horizontal, vertical or clean 45° stairs with one run length per stair; no stray pixel; no anti-aliasing on outer edges.** Sources: *Pixel Art Tutorial: Basics*; *Susan Kare's Macintosh icons and fonts*; *Pixel Logic: A Guide to Pixel Art*. Design system: **follows** (§13: the 45° stroke is a run of 3 on the stair).
22. **Silhouette first: every icon reads as a silhouette at 12 px; every picture passes a squint test.** Sources: *Pixel Art Tutorials (Saint11)*; *Pixel Art Tutorial: Basics*. Design system: **does not say** (not in §13 or the grill list).
23. **A fixed palette with hue-shifted ramps; art uses the palette's inks only.** Sources: *Pixelblog (64 tutorials)*; *A Dictionary of Color Combinations*; *Pixel Art Tutorial: Basics*. Design system: **follows** (§1, §13).
24. **Ma and kanso: every void is designed and measured in cells, every element has to defend itself, a surface at rest is calm and fills with meaning only when a state asks.** Sources: *Designing Design*; *White*; *Kenya Hara on Muji*; *MA: Espace-temps du Japon*; *Zen and the Fine Arts*; *The Book of Tea*. Design system: **partly** (§0 and §3 specify air and calm, but never as a principle and nothing says "remove until it hurts").
25. **Kire and shadow: separations are clean cuts (hairline, gap), never blur or gradient; the dark mood is dim, its brightest ink is not white.** Sources: *Japanese Aesthetics*; *In Praise of Shadows*; *Wabi-Sabi: for Artists, Designers, Poets & Philosophers*; *Ten principles of good design*. Design system: **follows** (§4 one hairline and a two-row shadow, no blur; §1 `strong` is `f1e1b4`).

### Menus, states and placement

26. **A popup opens directly under its tile and overlaps its width (a wide tunnel for the pointer); one at a time.** Sources: *Beyond Fitts' law: models for trajectory-based HCI tasks*; *A Quiz Designed to Give You Fitts*; *The information capacity of the human motor system in controlling the amplitude of movement*. Design system: **follows** (§6: top hairline on y 33, centred under the tile, 8 px inside the screen; the 8 px gap is the window gap).
27. **Choices per view are few: about five tabs or groups, about seven rows per group, a search line for anything longer.** Sources: *On the rate of gain of information*; *The magical number seven, plus or minus two*; *The magical number 4 in short-term memory*; *Laws of UX*. Design system: **partly** (§7 gives long lists a search line and short ones none; no numeric limits).
28. **State is visible where the thing is, by a shape or a word as well as colour.** Sources: *The Design of Everyday Things, revised and expanded*; *Refactoring UI*; *Contrast and Color Accessibility*. Design system: **follows** (§5: the chosen dot, `●` in TUIs, on/off words, hollow meter segments).
29. **One light at a time; a menu opens clean; no hidden modes.** Sources: *The Humane Interface*; *The Eight Golden Rules of Interface Design*; *10 Usability Heuristics for User Interface Design*. Design system: **follows** (§5 "one light, never two"; §6 "Opens clean").
30. **Feedback within 100 ms of input (hover within one frame), a busy icon after 1 s, and nothing ever waits for an animation.** Sources: *Response Times: The 3 Important Limits*; *Laws of UX*; *Measure performance with the RAIL model*; *Command Line Interface Guidelines*. Design system: **partly** (§6 and §11 say nothing waits or holds input; the 100 ms figure is not written).
31. **Keyboard first: any action in at most two keys, the same key does the same job on every screen, no footer of the obvious.** Sources: *The Psychology of Human-Computer Interaction*; *Common User Access: Basic Interface Design Guide*; *Command Line Interface Guidelines*. Design system: **follows** (§6 same key closes, §7 "No footer of the obvious").
32. **Smart, not chatty: no text that explains what the design makes obvious; labels are one or two lower-case words.** Sources: *Don't Make Me Think, Revisited*; *About Face: The Essentials of Interaction Design*; *Dieter Rams: As Little Design as Possible*; *Magic Ink*. Design system: **follows** (§2 lower case, §7 no footer).
33. **The keyboard cursor is the strongest mark on screen, always visible, never obscured.** Sources: *Web Content Accessibility Guidelines (WCAG) 2.2*; *The Eight Golden Rules of Interface Design*. Design system: **follows** (§4 the lacquer cursor at 7:1 words; §12 the focused window's `accent` hairline).

### Text screens (terminal)

34. **A terminal gap is optical: one blank line is about two columns of air, and in an 8 × 20 cell 2.5 columns equal one line.** Sources: *Textual — Layout guide*; *Lip Gloss*; *Ratatui — Layout concepts*. Design system: **does not say** (§7 gives the margin of one cell and blank lines around headings, not the 2.5 : 1 equivalence).
35. **Columns are counted by display width (wide = 2, ambiguous = 1), icons are two cells, strings are never measured by length.** Sources: *Unicode Standard Annex #11: East Asian Width*; *UTF-8 and Unicode FAQ for Unix/Linux*; *CJKV Information Processing*; *Notcurses*. Design system: **follows** (§2 and §7: Sumi Icons Term in two cells; the width tables).
36. **The 16 colour slots are ours and checked; NO_COLOR gives a readable monochrome; no hard-coded 256-colour numbers.** Sources: *NO_COLOR*; *Terminal colours are tricky*; *ECMA-48: Control Functions for Coded Character Sets*; *Command Line Interface Guidelines*. Design system: **partly** (§1 defines and checks the 16; NO_COLOR is not said).
37. **A screen is a pure function of its state, redrawn only where it changed, answering within 100 ms and never animating into a pipe.** Sources: *Bubble Tea*; *Command Line Interface Guidelines*; *Measure performance with the RAIL model*. Design system: **partly** (§11 "Only what changed is drawn again" is for the compositor; TUIs are not covered).

### Motion

38. **Durations come from a short ladder; entering is longer than leaving; ease-out in, ease-in or ease-in-out out, never linear, in whole pixels; nothing over about 400 ms except rare, large transitions.** Sources: *Executing UX Animations: Duration and Motion Characteristics*; *Easing and duration (tokens)*; *Motion, Tweening, and Easing*. Design system: **follows** (§11: popups 200/160, notices 240/200, windows 420/400, the 1.2 s wash only for a theme or wallpaper change, eased out in whole pixels).
39. **No overshoot; a stamp is a press, not a bounce; every motion has one stated purpose; decorative motion can be switched off while status changes remain.** Sources: *Designing Fluid Interfaces (WWDC18)*; *Motion (Apple HIG)*; *Designing Interface Animation*; *The Illusion of Life: Disney Animation*. Design system: **partly** (§11 gives each kind one meaning and eases out only; there is no reduce-motion switch).
40. **Timing is specified as frames at 144 Hz (whole frames and positions), not only in milliseconds.** Sources: *The Animator's Survival Kit*; *Principles of Traditional Animation Applied to 3D Computer Animation*. Design system: **partly** (§11 draws every refresh and says each step is exactly a refresh apart, but gives durations in ms: 420 ms is 60.5 frames).
41. **Objects stay traceable through a change and one thing moves at a time; overlap is follow-through.** Sources: *Animation: From Cartoons to the User Interface*; *Principles of Traditional Animation Applied to 3D Computer Animation*; *The Illusion of Life: Disney Animation*. Design system: **follows** (§11: a window dissolves while it rises 9 px; leaving and arriving windows overlap; no hard edge sweeps).
42. **Frame budget at 144 Hz is 6.94 ms; our own work stays near 4 ms (60 %, as RAIL does at 60 Hz) and input is handled within 50 ms.** Sources: *Measure performance with the RAIL model*; *Response Times: The 3 Important Limits*. Design system: **does not say** (the budget lives in the performance notes, not in the design system).
43. **The display is part of the design: the monitor profile is set so the palette's measured contrasts hold on the real glass, and every surface is judged in its real context in both moods.** Sources: *ISO 9241-303:2011 — Requirements for electronic visual displays*; *ISO 9241-125:2017 — Guidance on visual presentation of information*; *Designing with the Mind in Mind*; *Interaction of Color*. Design system: **does not say** (the monitor profile is a separate task; §15 asks for both moods side by side).

**Tally:** follows 21, partly 10, contradicts 2, does not say 10.

### The five places where the design system most clearly falls short

1. **Dark-mood contrast is judged only by the WCAG ratio (rule 14).** By APCA the dark mood's `text` is Lc 68, `muted` and `accent_text` Lc 38, below the floors for 14 px words, while the light mood passes; this matches the owner's complaint that highlighted contents in dark are not contrasty enough.
2. **No legibility floor (rule 13).** Sumi UI's x-height is exactly at the critical print size at 50 cm and below it at 60 cm; the document states no viewing distance and no minimum, so "a little smaller" has no limit.
3. **Pointer targets and edges (rule 10).** Rows and keys are 20 px high with no written exception, and the bar's 3 px of air above its hairline is not said to be clickable.
4. **Japanese beside Latin has no rule (rule 19).** No quarter-em junction, no half-width squeeze, no whole-character lines, although kana, kanji and Latin meet on the lock, the bar's seal and the launcher.
5. **The spacing list is not closed and the proximity ratio is not written (rules 2, 3).** Several numbers live outside §3, and nothing says a panel's height is rows × 20 + k × 8; the same gap shows the missing optical centre of screen-centred popups (rule 6) and the missing reduce-motion switch (rule 39).
